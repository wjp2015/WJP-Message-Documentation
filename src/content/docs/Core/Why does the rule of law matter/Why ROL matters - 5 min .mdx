Imagine a game where the rules are written down, but only some players have to follow them. One player can move the goalposts. Another can ignore the referee. A stronger player can push weaker players aside. And when someone breaks the rules, nothing happens.

Very quickly, people stop trusting the game. They stop playing fairly. Some give up. Others try to win by cheating too.

That is what societies look like when the rule of law is weak.

At its core, the rule of law is a simple but powerful idea: the rules should apply to everyone. Not only to ordinary people, but also to presidents, ministers, judges, police officers, companies, wealthy individuals, media owners, digital platforms, and powerful groups. No one should be able to make up the rules for themselves.

This is not about left or right, government or opposition, one party or another. It is about whether the basic rules that protect people’s lives still work. The rule of law means that power has limits, rights are protected, and people have somewhere to turn when they are treated unfairly.

## The rule of law matters because it limits power.

**First, it limits political power.** Public officials should not be able to use the state to enrich themselves, punish opponents, silence critics, weaken judges, manipulate information, or ignore court decisions simply because those decisions are inconvenient. Independent courts, legislatures, oversight institutions, anti-corruption bodies, elections, civil society, and a free press all help ensure that those who govern are also governed by law.

**Second, it limits economic power.** A large company should not be able to exploit workers, destroy the environment, break contracts, misuse personal data, manipulate markets, or crush smaller competitors simply because it has more money or influence. Clear rules, fair regulation, and accessible justice systems allow individuals, communities, workers, consumers, and businesses to defend their rights and compete on more equal terms.

**Third, it limits coercive power,** the power to use force, fear, or intimidation. Criminal groups, armed actors, or even state security forces should not be able to use violence without consequences. A society governed by the rule of law needs police who can protect people, courts that can punish crime, and due process that prevents the innocent from being abused in the name of security.

## This is why the rule of law is not an abstract legal concept. 
It shapes everyday life.
It matters when a citizen challenges a government decision and knows that the judge is independent. It matters when a journalist investigates corruption without fear of retaliation. It matters when people can trust that public information is not being manipulated to divide society, justify discrimination, or silence critics. It matters when a small business signs a contract and trusts that it will be enforced, even against a more powerful company. It matters when workers, consumers, or communities can use the law to protect themselves from exploitation or abuse. It matters when a woman is safe from violence in her home, on the street, or in her community — and when, if violence occurs, she can report it knowing that the police will protect her, the courts will hear her case, and the system will treat both victim and accused with fairness and due process.

Most people do not think about the rule of law every day. They think about groceries, school, work, health care, safety, rent, transportation, their business, their family, and whether they can build a better future. But all of those things depend, in quiet ways, on rules people can trust. 

**When the rule of law works, it creates trust.** People trust that rules will be applied fairly. Businesses trust that contracts will be honored. Communities trust that disputes can be resolved without violence. Citizens trust that government power has limits. That trust creates predictability. Predictability allows people to plan their lives, invest in their future, start businesses, hire workers, speak freely, and participate in public life. Over time, it supports prosperity, stability, and human dignity.

## But the rule of law can erode slowly. 
It does not always collapse overnight. In some countries, the signs are obvious: corruption, violence, arbitrary detention, weak courts, and impunity for powerful actors. In others, institutions may still function, but warning signs begin to appear: attacks on judges, efforts to weaken oversight institutions, pressure on the media, laws used to target opponents, public narratives that portray certain groups as less deserving of rights, or leaders who suggest that some people are above the law.

At first, the people affected may seem distant from your own life. They may be immigrants, protesters, journalists, judges, political opponents, minority groups, small business owners, workers, or communities without much influence. It can be tempting to think, “That is not about me.”

But the deeper risk is that power is becoming less constrained. Once leaders, companies, armed groups, or powerful interests learn that rules can be bent for some people, ignored by others, or applied only when convenient, the rules become less reliable for everyone.

That is why the rule of law matters everywhere — not only in fragile states and not only in democracies under crisis. It matters in every society where power exists, because power always needs limits.

The rule of law does not mean that every law is good. Bad laws can exist. Unjust laws can exist. But a strong rule of law system creates mechanisms to challenge, change, and correct them. It protects the space for people to speak, organize, litigate, vote, investigate, report, and hold power accountable.

**In the end, the rule of law is about whether people can live with dignity under rules that are fair, public, and applied to all.**

It is the difference between a society where power decides what is right, and a society where law limits power so that rights can be protected.

It is not only about courts, lawyers, or constitutions. It is about whether a family can feel safe, whether a worker can defend his rights, whether a business can plan for the future, whether a journalist can tell the truth, whether a woman can live free from violence, and whether an ordinary person can stand up to someone more powerful and still be treated fairly.

That is why the rule of law matters.
