<Aside title="We are living in a rule of law recession.">
For nearly a decade, the WJP has tracked a troubling global trend: the rule of law is weakening. More than 60% of countries we measure have seen their scores drop. The situation is serious — but it is not irreversible. The same data that documents this decline also shows that progress is possible. 
</Aside>

## Three-part formula
At WJP, we drive progress through a three-part formula:  **Evidence, Co-creation, and Local Action.** Evidence acts as the anchor; hard facts allow us to sideline politicized and polarized rhetoric. 


**Data is our starting point for difficult conversations to detonate change. We break down silos among stakeholders, foster trust, explore underlying obstacles and bottlenecks together, and align around a unified, collaborative strategy. We then focus on turning strategy into a binding policy that can ultimately improve people’s experience of the rule of law.**

As an independent organization, our role isn't to finger-point. Instead, we use evidence as an objective entry point to build trust, convene diverse stakeholders, and co-create practical solutions.

Let me show you what that looks like in practice. 

## Evidence
In 2018, we began measuring the rule of law at a subnational level in Mexico. The picture was mixed. Some states — particularly those near the top of the rankings — invested in improvement and saw results across six successive editions. Others stagnated. 
Quintana Roo — home to Cancún — was among the worst. Year after year, their score never moved. By the sixth edition, they sat at the bottom of the table. Then, they reached out.

## Co-creation
Evidence only creates value if it’s put to work. 
Because of our independent posture, we have a rare ability: we can bring government officials and civil society to the same table — and have both sides actually listen. We partnered with the government of Quintana Roo, training its ministries on how to interpret and apply the index findings. Together, we examined the institutional and political obstacles - not just the ones on paper- that were stalling the state. We prioritized needs, and endorsed local leaders to drive change. They explored solutions and held workshops that culminated in a formal public policy instrument. This policy instrument had a budget and it was legally binding. In other words, it had legs. Because of it, different government entities had the means and motivation to improve in identified areas. 

One issue stood out: due process — specifically, accountability mechanisms around torture and extrajudicial executions. 

When it comes to criminal conduct committed by state officials, governments are rarely open to improvement, we managed to earn their trust through three main catalysts: 
First, we shared a mutual goal of advancing the rule of law.
Second, they already knew us and respected our research and neutrality. 
Finally, we brought our convening power to the table —and they understood that for this initiative to succeed, they needed local NGO and activist buy-in. 

## Local Action
We worked hand in hand with local activists and the state prosecutor’s office to develop an operational roadmap with explicit performance milestones to tackle two urgent matters: impunity and distrust. We defined specific actions that needed to be taken by the prosecuting office, and paired these actions to civic oversight. We built a live tracking platform, hosted directly on the prosecution website, that empowers local civil society organizations to independently monitor progress, implementation, verify outcomes, and hold their government accountable.

Evidence without application is just a diagnosis. WJP closes the loop — from measurement to meaning to movement. We work with governments to turn findings into policy. With civil society to build accountability. With the private sector to make the case that strong institutions are the foundation of sustainable growth. In every case, the approach is the same: start with evidence, build the coalition, support the local leaders, and stay in it for the long haul. The rule of law doesn't break down overnight — and it doesn't get fixed that way either.
