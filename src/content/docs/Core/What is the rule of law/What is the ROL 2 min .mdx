When we talk about the rule of law, we are talking about something very simple: trust.
Trust that the rules are clear. Trust that they are fair. And trust that they apply to everyone — not only to ordinary citizens, but also to presidents, ministers, judges, police officers, companies, and powerful groups.

<Aside type="note" title="At its heart">
The rule of law means that power must answer to the law, no matter who holds that power.
</Aside>

It means that political power has limits. Public officials must serve the public, not themselves. It means that economic power has limits. Businesses should be able to compete, innovate, and grow — but on fair terms. And it means that coercive power has limits. Police and security forces must protect people, not intimidate them.

When the rule of law is strong, rights are protected, and justice does not depend on who you are, who you know, or how much influence you hold.

This does not happen by accident. It depends on institutions and people: independent courts, fair regulation, free elections, oversight bodies, civil society, a free press, and due process.
