Roughly two thirds of the people in this room live in a country where a judge fears ruling against the government because her career depends on it, where a journalist self-censors because speaking freely carries real risk, where the institutions meant to hold power accountable – institutions such as your senate, your congress, your parliament, your courts, your auditing agencies –   have instead become instruments of it. Two thirds of you live in country where corrupt officials don’t face repercussions, where you don’t feel safe in certain areas, or at certain times of the night, where you don’t raise your voice because you don’t trust the authorities. At least two thirds of you are living through a rule of law recession, and at its center is a single force: the unchecked expansion of executive power — in other words: Authoritarianism. 

Authoritarianism creeps in as checks and balances erode — through politicized courts, shrinking civic freedoms, and security forces that operate above the law. 
What keeps authoritarianism in check issn’t any one institution — it is a whole architecture of accountability: independent courts, transparent governments, protected civil society, and security forces that work for people rather than against them.  When that architecture breaks down, no one is safe from the abuse of power. A court whose budget depends on political goodwill cannot be impartial. A security institution that targets dissent or abuses human rights undermines the very order it claims to protect. 

Here's the problem. These institutions should have the public's defense, but they are being left to face executive attacks alone, because people have lost faith in them. Courts are perhaps the most visible casualty of this dynamic — and the most telling. Justice systems are facing a trust crisis.  Courts that once operated behind closed doors are now being asked to explain themselves — to communicate decisions in plain language, connect with their communities, earn back people's confidence.  In fact, access to laws and government information has remained stagnant worldwide (ROLI). But most don't know where to start. They don't even have a clear picture of how well — or badly — they're currently doing. 

The World Justice Project has prioritized countering authoritarianism. We help institutions take a look in the mirror, and then do something about it. We make the invisible visible: our indexes, regional tools such as WJP Eurovoices, and human rights dashboards in Asia give governments and civil society a clear, evidence-based picture of where accountability is failing and where reform must begin. These measurements are not just about cataloguing what's going wrong —  they also aim to identify best practices and instances of what is working, so that others may take note. Because change is possible, and our research proves it. 

In Mexico,WJP is working alongside local governments to strengthen due process and accountability mechanisms around torture and extrajudicial executions. Globally, through our Uphold Justice program, we’re identifying the real obstacles judges face in ruling freely and amplifying their stories through platforms such as our In the Robe podcast. 
The goal isn't just to document the problem. It's to build the kind of accountability infrastructure that makes authoritarianism harder to sustain — one institution, one judge, one community at a time.
