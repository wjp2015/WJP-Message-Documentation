## Dominican Republic - Justice Actors
*Justice as it is lived in everyday life*
Alejandro Ponce, April 2026
Audience: Justice Actors

Let me begin with a simple story.
During the December holidays, I spoke with a friend in Mexico. His family runs a construction company, and one of their main clients was the government. They completed their work and delivered their projects on time… but they have not been paid. Months have passed.
Before, they would have gone to court.
Today, that is much harder.
Following last year’s judicial reform, the system is slower and more uncertain. Cases are piling up. Outcomes are unpredictable.
So what happens?
Instead of investing, they are freezing projects.
Instead of hiring, they are cutting costs.
Instead of planning for the future, they are trying to survive.
This is not just a legal problem.
It is an economic and human problem.
And it is not an isolated case.
Similar situations are experienced by millions of people every day.
A neighbor takes over someone’s land… and nothing happens.
An employer does not pay, and a family is left without income… because they do not know where to turn.
A woman suffers violence… and there are no consequences, because the fear of revictimization outweighs the hope for justice.
Someone is detained simply for doing their job or speaking up.
For these people, these problems are not abstract. They are real.
When the law is not applied equally to everyone…
when there are no consequences…
when there is no response…
the idea that the law is enforced equally, that institutions protect rights, and that justice is done begins to fade.
In short, the sense that the rule of law exists starts to disappear.
And when that happens…
the law of the strongest begins to prevail,
abuse and violence increase,
corruption becomes normalized,
public services deteriorate,
investment declines,
and opportunities shrink for everyone.
 
**1. The rule of law matters—and it is under pressure**
Today we know something clearly:
The rule of law is under pressure in many parts of the world.
Data from the World Justice Project shows that over the past decade, the rule of law has weakened in most countries, driven by declining checks and balances, shrinking civic space, and growing demand for justice that systems are unable to meet.
But we also know something else.
There is progress.
There are examples.
And today, we are in one of them.
In the latest edition of the Rule of Law Index, the Dominican Republic was the top global improver among 143 countries, with a 2.1% increase, including gains in checks and balances, control of corruption, and justice.
In 2025, the country moved up 11 positions.
Between 2021 and 2025, the Dominican Republic recorded the largest improvement in rule of law globally, with a 5.4% increase—compared to an average decline of -1.8% worldwide. During this period, the country rose 21 positions.
This is not by chance.
It is the result of decisions.
Of reforms to strengthen anti-corruption mechanisms,
modernize judicial processes,
expand access to legal services,
and promote greater transparency.
Much remains to be done. The Dominican Republic still ranks 76 out of 143 countries.
But something fundamental is in place:
There is direction.
There is commitment.
There is progress.
Change is possible.
 
**2. What does change look like?**

To achieve this change, we must work on multiple fronts. And this is where institutions—especially the justice system—play a fundamental role:
to prevent abuse,
to correct it when it occurs,
to apply the law,
to protect rights,
to strengthen trust,
and to deliver justice.
You already know what this means—what it takes to build:
First, an impartial and independent justice system,
where decisions are made without pressure,
with integrity,
and with equality before the law.
Second, a people-centered, effective, and high-quality justice system,
that addresses both everyday problems and cases of violence and crime,
that is accessible,
that resolves cases in a timely manner,
with sound decisions,
and with coordination across institutions.
And third, an open and trustworthy justice system,
that is transparent, participatory,
and accountable.
These are the core components of the rule of law.
They represent the real capacity to solve people’s legal problems.
They are the foundation of legitimacy and trust.
Because ultimately, justice is not measured in case files… but in how it impacts people’s lives.
Achieving this change is not easy, and there is no single formula—but we do have clear lessons.
Looking at positive experiences around the world—including what we are seeing in the Dominican Republic—we can identify three key actions:
First, understand the experience of those who face legal problems or are victims of abuse or violence.
What do they do?
What are their fears and frustrations?
What discourages them from seeking justice?
What difficulties do they face when interacting with institutions?
What makes them feel the system does not work for them?
What do they expect from it?
Listen.
Analyze.
And respond based on that reality.
Second, show leadership and commitment.
Changing systems means confronting inertia.
Making difficult decisions.
Staying the course over time.
And building trust within the system itself.
And third, act collectively and in a coordinated way.
Change requires many actors—
judges, prosecutors, lawyers, academics, businesses, and civil society.
It requires understanding that change is systemic.
And that transforming justice is a shared effort.


 
**3. You are the agents of change**

This is where you come in.
Because you are not just part of the system.
You are the system.
Every decision you make.
Every person you serve.
Every explanation you give—or do not give.
Every time you allow or stop an abuse.
Every time you ensure accountability.
All of this defines how justice is experienced.
You have something extraordinary:
The ability to change someone’s experience at a critical moment.
To make the system clearer,
more trustworthy,
more humane,
and more just.
 
To close, let me return to the beginning.
That person who simply wants to be paid what they are owed,
who wants to feel safe,
who wants to be heard.
They are not thinking about major reforms.
They are thinking about something much simpler:
Will someone help me solve my problem?
Will I be treated with respect?
Can I trust the system?
The rule of law lives—or dies—in that experience.
This is a profound responsibility.
And also, a great opportunity.
The opportunity to build a system that works.
That is dignified.
And that truly responds to people.
You have shown that change is possible—and through your daily work, you continue to build that story.
Thank you very much.


## Thailand - Justice Actors
*Building a New Leadership for the Rule of Law*
Alejandro Ponce, June 2026
Audience: Justice Actors

Let me begin by thanking the Thailand Institute of Justice. WJP has been proud to work with TIJ and with many partners in Thailand over the years. Together, we have supported policy dialogues, strengthened the use of evidence, and helped create space for serious conversations about justice and governance.

That role matters. The rule of law is too broad, and too important, for any one institution to strengthen alone. It requires government, business, civil society, academia, and communities working together. TIJ has helped create that space, and we are grateful for its leadership.

**The rule of law is often discussed in legal or institutional terms. But at its core, it is about something very simple: trust.**

People need to trust that rules are clear, fair, and applied to everyone. They need to trust that public officials cannot use power for personal or political purposes. They need to trust that businesses can compete fairly, that rights will be protected, and that disputes can be resolved without fear or violence.
When that trust is strong, societies can plan, invest, grow, and solve problems peacefully.
When that trust is weak, people begin to lose confidence in institutions. They may feel that the rules protect only the powerful, or that justice depends on who you are, who you know, or how much influence you have.
That is why the rule of law matters.

It matters when a small business signs a contract and trusts it will be enforced. It matters when a journalist investigates corruption without fear. It matters when a woman can report violence and know that the police will protect her and the courts will hear her case. It matters when a citizen challenges a government decision and believes the judge will be independent.

Most people do not think about the rule of law every day. They think about their families, their work, their safety, their business, and their future. But all of those things depend, in quiet ways, on rules people can trust.

**And today, that trust is under pressure around the world.**

The WJP Rule of Law Index shows that the rule of law has weakened in many countries over the past decade. The latest data show that the rule of law declined in 68 percent of countries in 2025. This decline has been driven by weaker checks and balances, shrinking civic space, and justice systems that are not meeting people’s needs.

But even in difficult times, there are reasons for hope.

Thailand’s experience tells an important story.
According to the 2025 WJP Rule of Law Index, Thailand’s overall score is 0.50, ranking 77th out of 143 countries. Over the past five years, the overall picture shows limited progress. But beneath that number, there are two stories.

First, Thailand experienced setbacks after the pandemic. That is not unique. Many countries faced political, economic, and institutional stress during that period.

Second, since 2023, Thailand has shown renewed improvement. The overall rule of law score improved in 2024 and again in 2025. Constraints on government powers improved. Fundamental rights improved. Civil justice continued to make progress. Criminal justice also improved in the most recent year.

Over five years, civil justice has improved by about 5 percent. Order and security has also improved by about 5 percent since 2020. And some aspects of fundamental rights have improved by as much as 10 percent.

These are meaningful gains.

At the same time, important challenges remain. Absence of corruption has declined by about 8 percent over this period. Open government has declined by about 5 percent. These areas require renewed attention.

This mixed picture reminds us of an important truth: strengthening the rule of law is never a straight line. Progress is uneven. Some reforms move faster than others. Some institutions change before others do. Resources are limited. Incentives are complex. And not everyone welcomes reform.

But that is not a reason to step back.
It is a reason to lead.
**So how do we move forward?**

First, we need a strategic vision centered on people.
Rule of law reform is broad and deep. There are always many problems to solve, many ideas to consider, and many projects to launch. But success does not come from doing everything. It comes from knowing what matters most, setting clear goals, and staying focused.
That means supporting public policies that create fair legal frameworks, strengthen institutions, and deliver practical programs that improve people’s lives.
Thailand’s journey toward OECD accession creates an important window of opportunity. It can support legal and institutional reform. But accession should not become a box-ticking exercise. The real question is not only whether laws are passed. The real question is whether reforms increase trust, improve justice, and make institutions work better for people.

Second, we need greater cooperation and coordination.
Strengthening the rule of law is a systems challenge. It requires courts, regulators, police, legislators, businesses, civil society, educators, and communities to move in the same direction.

One encouraging development in Thailand is that efforts are becoming more coordinated. A few years ago, many initiatives existed in isolation. Different institutions were working hard, but often without a shared framework. Today, we see more collaboration, more openness to evidence, and a stronger willingness to work across institutions.
That is progress. And it should continue.

Third, we need commitment.
Changing systems means facing inertia. It means making difficult decisions. It means staying the course when reform becomes complicated, when results take time, and when resistance appears.
Leadership for the rule of law is not only about speaking in favor of reform. It is about building trust inside institutions. It is about making decisions that are fair, transparent, and accountable. It is about showing, through action, that power can be exercised with limits and with responsibility.
This brings me to all of you.
You are already leaders. Some of you serve in government. Some lead businesses. Some work in civil society, academia, or communities. Many of you will assume even greater responsibilities in the years ahead.
Leadership in the rule of law is not about quick victories. It requires integrity. It requires discipline. It requires courage. And it requires the commitment to build change that lasts.

This is also where the partnership between TIJ and the World Justice Project can make an important contribution.

Our shared goal is to support Thailand’s own efforts to strengthen the rule of law in practical and lasting ways. Together, we can help build the evidence base for reform; support institutions as they identify priorities and strengthen their capacity; and create spaces for policy dialogue and public understanding.

That work matters because evidence helps clarify where progress is being made and where more attention is needed. Institutional support helps translate priorities into action. Dialogue helps build the trust and cooperation that reform requires.

Ultimately, this partnership is about supporting Thailand’s path toward stronger institutions, greater public trust, and a rule of law system that works better for people.
Because the future of the rule of law in Thailand will not be built by one institution, one reform, or one moment.

It will be built by leaders who choose fairness over convenience. Evidence over assumption. Cooperation over fragmentation. And public trust over short-term gain.

That is the leadership this moment requires.

Thank you very much, and I wish you every success on this journey.
